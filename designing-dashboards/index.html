Why this matters now

AI makes dashboards fast. It also makes bad ones fast.

Most dashboards don't fail loudly. They launch, get opened twice, and quietly die. The causes are almost always the same three.

1

Built for data, not decisions

Every metric goes on the page because it exists, not because anyone acts on it.

2

Too much, too clever

Twenty charts, five colours, three fonts. The one number that matters gets lost.

3

Numbers nobody trusts

No definitions, no source, no date. One wrong figure and people return to spreadsheets.

The frameworks in this playbook give you the right dashboard: decades of research on how people read charts and make decisions. AI gives you that dashboard built and checked, faster, once you tell it what good looks like.

3Keep this open

The field guide.

Sahayak showed the loop in motion. These are the reference pages to come back to while you build your own: the whole loop on one screen, the four kinds of dashboard, the metric card and the layout rules.

D

Decide

Who decides what, how often?

  • Write: this helps [who] decide [what] every [cadence]
  • Pick the kind: strategic, operational, analytical or communicative
  • List three to five questions in the readers' words
  • Interview two real readers first

Stands on Patton's utilization-focused evaluation, Munzner's domain level and jobs to be done.

E

Establish metrics

Which numbers answer those questions, exactly?

  • Derive metrics from your theory of change, logframe or OKRs
  • One card per metric: definition, formula, source, owner
  • Pair each lagging outcome with a leading indicator
  • Plan equity breakdowns before collecting data

Stands on SMART indicators, results chains and Few's warning about deficient measures.

S

Structure the layout

What does the reader see first, second, third?

  • Inverted pyramid: headline, KPIs, trends, breakdowns, detail
  • Core view on one screen
  • Most important number top left
  • Group related items; same metric, same place

Stands on Shneiderman's mantra, Few's single screen, Gestalt and Z and F reading.

I

Illustrate

Which chart shows each relationship most clearly?

  • Choose charts by relationship
  • Bars start at zero; label directly
  • Grey everything, colour only what matters
  • Titles state the takeaway

Stands on the FT Visual Vocabulary, Tufte's data-ink, Knaflic and pre-attentive attributes.

G

Guard

Can everyone read it, and trust it?

  • Contrast 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for marks
  • Never encode meaning in colour alone
  • Show source, freshness and definitions
  • De-identify; suppress small groups

Stands on WCAG 2.2, the Okabe and Ito palette and do-no-harm data ethics.

N

Nurture

Is it used, and getting better?

  • Test with five readers on three tasks
  • Track views, filters and questions
  • Review quarterly; retire what nobody uses
  • Version it and log changes

Stands on usability testing, Few's thirteen mistakes and Munzner's validation.

Mixing these on one page is the most common design mistake of all. If you need two, build two pages. Tap a kind to see what it looks like.

Metric card, Sahayak example

On-time service rate

Question
Are we serving people within the promised time?
Definition
Share of requests closed within 7 days of receipt
Formula
closed_within_7d ÷ requests_closed
Source
Case system export, refreshed weekly
Type
Lagging outcome; its leading indicator is backlog size
Target
85% by December, set with the operations lead
Breakdowns
Region, request type, gender of applicant
Owner
Operations lead; acts when below 75% two weeks running
  1. Start from the results chain. Metrics come from your theory of change, logframe or OKRs, not from what the database holds.
  2. Pair leading with lagging. Outcomes tell you if you won; leading indicators tell you in time to change course.
  3. Every metric needs an action. If nobody would act differently when it moves, it belongs in an appendix.
  4. Plan breakdowns up front. Decide equity cuts now so the data is collected to support them.
  5. Context, always. Show each number against a target, the last period or a comparison group.

Hand your AI the theory of change and the data dictionary, and ask for six of these cards. Then ask which ones it could not compute from your data; that list is the real finding.

    Pre-attentive attributes

    The eye spots colour, size, position, length and enclosure before reading a word. Use one of them, once, on the thing that matters.

    Graphical integrity

    Bars start at zero, scales stay consistent across small multiples, and areas are never used for one-dimensional values.

    Colour check

    About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour vision deficiency. Switch the view, then switch the palette.

    5Working with AI
    
      
    The AI pact.
    
      
    AI can draft almost everything on a dashboard. It cannot be accountable for any of it. Here is how to split the work: eight jobs it does well, what you keep in each, and six promises that hold whatever the tool.
    
    
    
    
    Eight roles AI can play. One it can't.
    
    
    AI is a fast, tireless collaborator. It is not accountable for your numbers. You are.
    
    
    
      
    1
    Interviewer
    Surfaces purpose, readers and decisions.
    You keep: confirming with real users
    
      
    2
    Metric architect
    Turns questions into metric cards.
    You keep: signing off definitions
    
      
    3
    Layout sketcher
    Drafts wireframes and reading order.
    You keep: choosing what to cut
    
      
    4
    Chart chooser
    Matches relationships to charts.
    You keep: the final visual call
    
      
    5
    Builder
    Writes DAX, SQL, formulas or chart code.
    You keep: recomputing the headlines
    
      
    6
    Critic
    Reviews against Few, Tufte and Knaflic.
    You keep: weighing trade-offs
    
      
    7
    Accessibility checker
    Flags contrast, colour-only meaning and jargon.
    You keep: testing with a contrast tool
    
      
    8
    Narrator
    Writes takeaways, summaries and alt text.
    You keep: checking every claim
    
    
    
    
    The one it can't play: the person who answers for the numbers.
    
    
    Six promises to keep.
    
    
    
      
    1
    Every number traces to a row
    Nothing on the page you can't follow back to source data and a formula.
    
      
    2
    Headlines get recomputed
    The top three figures are checked by a person before anyone else sees them.
    
      
    3
    No personal data goes in
    Aggregate and de-identify first, unless your organisation has approved the tool.
    
      
    4
    Definitions come first
    AI fills a gap with a plausible definition. Give it yours.
    
      
    5
    A person signs off
    Each dashboard has a named owner for numbers, wording and release.
    
      
    6
    AI's help is disclosed
    One line on the page: what AI drafted, what people checked.
