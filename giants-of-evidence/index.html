02 · Why people

Why learn MEL through people?

Most of us meet evaluation as a set of finished tools: a logframe template, a theory of change workshop, a sampling formula, a story collection form. They arrive without their history, so they can feel arbitrary, and we use them mechanically.

Every one of those tools began as somebody's answer to a real problem. An evaluator needed to show whether experimental schools still prepared students for college. A field worker in Bangladesh needed to summarise progress without a fixed list of indicators. Economists working with an Indian NGO wanted to know whether a remedial tutoring programme actually helped children learn. When you know the problem behind a tool, you know when to use it, when to bend it and when to put it down.

People also make ideas memorable. It is easier to recall a person's turning point than a definition, and easier to argue with a thinker than with a box on a form. This book uses 24 stories to help you hold the field in your head as a living conversation, with disagreements, rather than a list of methods.

How to use this book

  1. Read a storyEach profile opens with a short, factual story in three parts: how the person came to evaluation, the turning point, and what changed because of them. Read one a day or one a week. The order is roughly chronological, but every profile stands alone.
  2. Try the toolEach profile turns the big idea into practice: three steps to use it on Monday, a fill-in template and an AI prompt you can copy. Use them on a live assignment, not a hypothetical one. The prompts are built to stop the AI from inventing evidence; keep it that way.
  3. ReflectEnd with the reflection question and the links to other figures. The connections show where ideas agree, extend or argue with each other. Write your answer down; over time your notes become your own practice philosophy.

Lessons, advice, templates and prompts are HubForge's own reading of each person's ideas. They are not claims about what the person said or would recommend.

He set out the logic in 1949 in Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction, and is widely credited with bringing the word evaluation into the vocabulary of schooling.

His research assistant in 1938, Lee Cronbach, went on to transform measurement itself, with coefficient alpha in 1951 and construct validity, with Paul Meehl, in 1955. In 1963 Cronbach turned back to evaluation and argued that it does most good while a course is still being developed, feeding improvement, rather than only judging a finished product. By the mid 1960s new federal funding was producing ambitious curriculum reforms, yet evaluations often reported little beyond average test scores. In 1967 two answers appeared. Michael Scriven named formative and summative evaluation and argued, against Cronbach, that judging overall worth mattered as much as supporting improvement. Robert Stake argued that a full evaluation must both describe and judge, comparing what was intended with what was observed. In 1972 Scriven went further still, proposing that evaluators could set stated goals aside and search for all of a programme's effects.

In our reading, this first act moves from a single question, did pupils reach the objectives, to a harder one: were these the right objectives, and what else happened along the way? Every logframe and every results framework in use today still lives inside that tension.

In the 1960s evaluation left the classroom and entered government. The Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 brought federal money to American schools, and an amendment demanded by Senator Robert Kennedy required evaluation of its Title I programmes. Local educators were often unprepared, and that demand, the Encyclopedia of Evaluation records, helped drive the development of many evaluation models. The tools to hand had been built for testing pupils, not for judging whole policies.

Donald Campbell supplied a discipline for judging causes in the field. In 1963, with Julian Stanley, he catalogued sixteen designs and listed eight threats to internal validity and four to external validity. In 1969, in Reforms as Experiments, he turned the toolkit on public policy, showing how a Connecticut claim about a speeding crackdown ignored trends and ordinary year-to-year swings. Daniel Stufflebeam, a test developer suddenly advising on federal evaluation, built the CIPP model, first presented in Austin in 1966, which treated evaluation as a service to decisions at every stage of a programme's life. Carol Weiss, whose 1972 textbook was among the first in the field, asked why findings seemed to be ignored, and in 1979 described seven ways research enters policy, including the slow enlightenment of debate. Huey-tsyh Chen, working with Peter Rossi, argued in 1983 that experiments treated programmes as black boxes, and in 1990 set out theory-driven evaluation.

In 1991 Shadish, Cook and Leviton grouped Campbell with Scriven as theorists who sought to bring truth to social problem solving, and Weiss with Stake and Joseph Wholey as theorists who emphasised use and pragmatism. In our reading, this act answered the first by scaling up its ambition: from whether pupils learned to whether a society could learn from its own reforms.

Others carried the idea inside organisations and into complexity. In 1999 Hallie Preskill and Rosalie Torres described evaluative inquiry, in which an organisation's own members continually examine what they do as part of daily work. Sanjeev Sridharan and colleagues proposed in 2006 that stakeholders set out when a programme should first be expected to show impact, and in 2011 he and Nakaima set out ten steps to make evaluation matter, arguing that a programme's complexity should shape its evaluation's design. Patricia Rogers, introduced to program logic at an evaluation conference in Canberra in 1987, argued in 2008 that evaluators should classify aspects of an intervention as simple, complicated or complex, and in 2011, with Sue Funnell, presented programme theory as a family of tools chosen to fit purpose.

In Alkin and Christie's evaluation theory tree, Stufflebeam, Patton and Preskill sit on the use branch. In our reading, this act answered the experimenting society with a practical rejoinder: a rigorous finding that no one acts on has helped no one, so the evaluator's craft must include the people and organisations who will use what is learned.

Many Voices, Many Truths

Far from American campuses, a parallel challenge was growing. Robert Chambers showed in 1983 how short official visits to villages were biased towards roadsides, better off people and the dry season, and in 1994 he documented participatory rural appraisal, in which rural people map and analyse their own realities while the outsider facilitates. In Bangladesh in 1994 the Christian Commission for Development in Bangladesh first used the technique Rick Davies designed, which Jess Dart later trialled in Australia and which became Most Significant Change: a way to collect stories of change and choose openly among them. In 1998 Donna Mertens led the American Evaluation Association under the theme of transforming society through evaluation, and went on to build a transformative paradigm that starts from power and justice.

In our reading, this act answered the use revolution by asking whose use, and whose truth. It widened the field's circle of legitimate knowers, from experts to participants, and made the evaluator's own values part of the question.

Marco Segone helped launch EvalPartners in 2012 to strengthen national evaluation associations, and in December 2014 the UN General Assembly adopted a resolution on country-level evaluation capacity. In South Asia, Sonal Zaveri helped found the Community of Evaluators in 2008 and later led a real-time, gender-focused evaluation during the COVID-19 response.

In 2012 Fred Carden and Marvin Alkin observed that evaluation theory from low and middle income countries seemed to have collective, practice-based origins rather than individual authors. In our reading, this act moves the field's centre of gravity to where programmes happen, and asks that evidence be both rigorous and owned.

After 2026

What comes next: AI and evaluation

The story does not stop in 2026. Artificial intelligence is arriving in evaluation work: drafting tools, coding interviews, summarising evidence. We do not know yet how it will reshape the field. The people in this book left us questions worth asking of it.

  1. Threats to validity (Campbell): when an AI tool summarises interviews or cleans data, what new rival explanations and biases does it introduce, and how would we detect them?
  2. Intended use (Patton): who are the intended users of an AI-generated finding, and do they understand how it was produced?
  3. Trustworthiness (Guba and Lincoln): what would it take to show that AI-assisted qualitative analysis can be trusted, and who should judge?
  4. Whose reality counts (Chambers, Chilisa, Ofir): if AI tools learn mostly from text produced in the Global North, whose knowledge do they amplify and whose do they miss?
  5. Equity (Segone, Mertens): who gains access to AI-enabled evaluation, and who is left behind?
  6. Evidence ecologies (White, Sridharan): can AI help decision-makers see whole bodies of evidence, or will it flood them with plausible text that has no evidence behind it?

These are open questions, not predictions. Every AI prompt in this book is written to stop the tool from inventing evidence; treat that as a minimum standard, not a guarantee.

07 · Great debates

Five arguments that shaped the field

Evaluation grew through argument. Each debate sets out both sides fairly, says what happened, and asks where you stand.

Great debate 1 of 5 · Act 2

Proof Here or Promise Elsewhere?

Should evaluation first prove that a programme caused an effect, or show whether the effect will travel?

See both sides and what happened

Side A

Internal validity first (Campbell)

Campbell's starting point was that a claim of programme success means little if rival explanations have not been ruled out. In 1963, with Julian Stanley, he catalogued sixteen designs and eight threats to internal validity, giving field researchers a shared language for what a comparison could and could not prove. In 1969, in Reforms as Experiments, he urged governments to treat reforms as experiments, and showed how a governor's claim about a speeding crackdown ignored trends and ordinary year-to-year swings. The Encyclopedia of Evaluation records that Campbell continued to prioritise internal validity. In our reading, his case is simple and powerful: before asking whether a result will transfer, be sure the result is real.

Side B

External validity and many studies (Cronbach)

Cronbach argued that the question policymakers most need answered is whether a programme that succeeded in one place will succeed in another, with different people and conditions. In Designing Evaluations of Educational and Social Programs (1982) he set out his units, treatments, observations and settings framework to address exactly that. According to the Encyclopedia of Evaluation, he opposed relying on one large experiment, which he likened to a horse race, and favoured many smaller studies, each looking closely at an important case or component. He saw evaluation as political and its main audience as a wide policy-shaping community. In our reading, his case is that a clean answer to a narrow question can still mislead a busy decision-maker.

What happened

Neither side simply won. In 1991 Shadish, Cook and Leviton placed Campbell among the stage one theorists who sought to bring truth to social problem solving, and Cronbach among the stage three theorists who tried to integrate what came before. In Evaluation Roots (2004), Thomas Cook wrote a chapter on how both men shaped his own theory of causal generalisation. Chen, who had criticised black box experiments with Peter Rossi in 1983, proposed in 2010 a bottom-up approach to integrative validity. In our reading, the same argument resurfaced in development economics after 2010, when critics asked whether trial results transfer across settings.

Where you stand

  1. Think of a pilot in your organisation that showed good results. What would you need to know before recommending it for a different district or state?
  2. If you had budget for one large study or five small ones, which would you choose for your next evaluation, and why?

Great debate 2 of 5 · Act 1

Goals or No Goals?

Should a programme be judged against its own stated objectives, or against everything it actually does?

See both sides and what happened

Side A

Start from objectives (Tyler)

Tyler's approach began with purposes. In the Eight-Year Study, rather than impose one standard test on very different schools, he and his staff worked from each school's own stated aims, clarified what each would look like in students' work, and built varied instruments to gather evidence on them. He distilled this into the 1949 Basic Principles of Curriculum and Instruction. The strength of the approach is fairness and clarity: a programme is held to what it set out to do, in terms its own staff understand, and evidence is gathered on more than test scores. In our reading, every results framework that asks a programme to define its intended outcomes and then measure them descends from this logic.

Side B

Look for all effects (Scriven, with Stake)

Critics argued that judging a programme only against its own objectives can miss unintended effects and cannot say whether the objectives were worth pursuing. In Pros and Cons About Goal-Free Evaluation (1972), Scriven drew on the way consumer product testers judge goods without asking manufacturers what they intended, and proposed that evaluators set stated goals aside and search for all of a programme's effects. Robert Stake, from a different angle, argued in 1967 that a full evaluation must both describe and judge, comparing intentions with what was actually observed, and later developed responsive evaluation around the issues people at a site care about. In our reading, their shared case is that goals can become blinkers.

What happened

Goal-free evaluation never became the norm. Brandon Youker, who has written extensively on the approach, reports that most evaluators treat it as a rhetorical reminder rather than a workable design, partly because strict blinding to goals is hard to achieve. Yet its central worry spread. In Christie's account of the second edition of the theory tree, goal-free evaluation sits on the objectivist side of the valuing branch, and Stake's responsive evaluation underlies most theories on the subjectivist side. Most Significant Change was designed partly because predefined indicators risked missing the unexpected. Tyler did not formally reply to his critics.

Where you stand

  1. In your last evaluation, what was the most important effect that nobody had written into the logframe?
  2. Could you design one part of your next evaluation in which the field team is not told the programme's objectives? What would you gain and lose?

Great debate 3 of 5 · Act 3

Use or Judgement?

Is an evaluation's first duty to be used by the people who need it, or to deliver an independent verdict on worth?

See both sides and what happened

Side A

Judge evaluation by its use (Patton)

Patton built his approach on evidence. A 1977 field study by Patton and colleagues found that findings were most likely to be used when an identifiable person cared about the evaluation, which he called the personal factor. Utilization-Focused Evaluation (1978) argued that evaluations should therefore be judged by their actual use and organised around specific primary intended users and their intended uses. In a 1996 issue of Evaluation Practice he argued that the world of evaluation was larger than the formative and summative distinction, pointing to developmental and knowledge-generating work. In our reading, his case is that an evaluation nobody uses is a cost without a benefit, however rigorous it is.

Side B

Evaluation means judging merit and worth (Scriven)

Scriven saw evaluation as the determination of value, and held that a competent evaluator can establish a programme's worth as a matter of fact rather than as a matter of taste. It was Scriven who in 1967 named formative and summative evaluation and insisted that judging overall worth was as important as supporting improvement. In the same 1996 issue of Evaluation Practice, he replied to Patton and others by defending the formative and summative distinction as one reasonable way to classify evaluation. A recurring concern in the field is that a focus on primary intended users can tilt an evaluation towards what powerful users want to know.

What happened

Both positions became pillars of the field. In Alkin and Christie's evaluation theory tree, Patton's work sits on the use branch and Scriven's at the base of the valuing branch, and the 2023 third edition of Evaluation Roots still gives both their own chapters. Patton went on to develop developmental evaluation, whose early adopters, in Mark Cabaj's 2011 study, wrestled with exactly this tension: whether to judge, and to whom they are accountable. Weiss's earlier work had already shown that use itself takes many forms, from direct application to slow enlightenment.

Where you stand

  1. Who are the primary intended users of the evaluation you are working on now, and have you asked them what they will do with the findings?
  2. When a funder and a community want different things from an evaluation, whose question comes first for you?

Great debate 4 of 5 · Act 4

The Paradigm Wars

Do qualitative and quantitative evaluation rest on incompatible views of reality, or can they be combined?

See both sides and what happened

Side A

A different paradigm with its own criteria (Guba and Lincoln)

Guba, a statistician who had worked inside the measurement tradition, came to doubt that conventional approaches could answer evaluation questions well. In Naturalistic Inquiry (1985) he and Lincoln argued that the naturalistic paradigm rests on different assumptions from conventional science, so studies within it should be judged by parallel criteria of trustworthiness rather than by internal and external validity. In 1986 they added criteria of authenticity, and in 1989 Fourth Generation Evaluation built an approach around negotiation among stakeholders. They advised against mixing positivist and constructivist propositions in a single study, while insisting that relativism did not mean anything goes. In our reading, their case is that methods carry worldviews.

Side B

The experimental tradition replies

Critics from the experimental tradition questioned whether a relativist position could yield findings that decision-makers could rely on, and whether parallel criteria could carry the weight of validity. William Shadish argued in 1995 that few evaluation theorists or practising evaluators were in fact logical positivists, a movement he described as largely dead in philosophy by the late 1940s, and that method choice often does not follow directly from philosophy. He also argued that experiments can include qualitative observation. Campbell himself, in Shadish's account, had written in support of qualitative work. In our reading, their case is that the war was fought partly against a caricature.

What happened

The field moved towards combination rather than conquest. In 1989 Greene, Caracelli and Graham reviewed 57 mixed-method evaluations and named five purposes for mixing methods. In 1997 Greene and Caracelli described purist, pragmatic and dialectic stances, the last treating tensions between paradigms as a source of fuller understanding. Mertens later built her transformative paradigm on the four philosophical assumptions Guba and Lincoln had identified. Shadish took Denzin and Lincoln's 1994 Handbook of Qualitative Research as a sign that qualitative methods had become established. The argument over paradigms continues in quieter form.

Where you stand

  1. In your current work, do numbers and stories answer different questions, or are they used to confirm each other? Which of Greene's purposes are you really serving?
  2. Is there a finding you trust from interviews that you could not defend to a sceptical statistician? How would you make your case?

Great debate 5 of 5 · Act 5

The Randomistas and Their Critics

Are randomised trials the best route to knowing what works against poverty, or one tool among many?

See both sides and what happened

Side A

Break big questions into testable ones (Banerjee and Duflo)

The Nobel committee's 2019 account summarised the approach: break the large question of global poverty into smaller, precise questions, such as which interventions best improve schooling or child health, and answer them through carefully designed experiments among the people most affected. In their 2009 review of the experimental approach, Banerjee and Duflo argued that its main advantage is close collaboration between researchers and implementers, which allows questions to be tested that could not otherwise be evaluated. They took the criticisms seriously but concluded that many were not specific to experiments. In his Nobel lecture Banerjee argued that replication across sites, combined with theory, can support generalisation.

Side B

No special access to truth (Deaton, Stern, Ofir)

Angus Deaton argued in the Journal of Economic Literature in 2010 that randomised trials and quasi-experiments give no special access to credible knowledge, that they often fail to handle heterogeneous effects, and that evaluating projects without understanding mechanisms is unlikely to advance knowledge of development. With Nancy Cartwright he later argued that results strictly describe the trial sample and that trials should be combined with other methods and theory. A 2012 DFID working paper led by Elliot Stern concluded that experimental designs suit only a minority of programmes. In Africa, Zenda Ofir and Sulley Gariba judged that such designs did not reflect African ways of knowing and could oversimplify complex communal societies.

What happened

Both camps reshaped practice. Banerjee, Duflo and Michael Kremer received the 2019 Nobel prize, and the committee credited one of their Indian studies with leading to remedial tutoring that has benefited more than five million children. Deaton himself welcomed a shift in development experiments from project evaluation towards testing mechanisms. Howard White argued in 2009 that impact evaluations should trace the full causal chain, and the Stern paper pressed for a broader range of designs. Ofir's concern helped spark Made in Africa Evaluation, which Chilisa developed into theory. The debate continues.

Where you stand

  1. For the programme you know best, what is the one question a randomised trial could answer well, and the one it could not?
  2. If a trial found an average effect of zero, what would you want to know about who gained and who lost before you advised ending the programme?

Methods

Theories that emphasise research design, causal inference and generalisation. One of the three original branches of Alkin and Christie's evaluation theory tree; Christie (2024) describes Campbell's work as foundational to it. A position on a branch shows relative emphasis, not exclusive focus (Mark 2024).

Use

Theories that emphasise how evaluation serves decisions, intended users and organisational learning. An original branch of the tree; in the 2004 book its part includes Stufflebeam, Wholey, Patton, Alkin, Fetterman, Cousins, King, Preskill and Owen.

Valuing

Theories that emphasise how evaluators reach judgements of merit and worth. An original branch; in the second edition it was split into an objectivist side, rooted in Scriven, and a subjectivist side, rooted in Stake (Christie 2024).

Social justice

A fourth branch added by Donna Mertens, aligned with the transformative paradigm (Mertens and Wilson 2012). Mark (2024) notes that Alkin and Christie's most recent version instead places more of these theories on the valuing branch. We use it here for evaluators who put power, equity and participation first.

Context and needs (proposed fifth branch)

A proposed branch, not part of Alkin and Christie's tree. Mertens (2023) proposed a fifth branch named context and needs, fitting an Indigenous paradigm. Chilisa (2024) describes the case for it, made in her own work and with Mertens, as drawing on the culture, philosophy, history and experience of formerly colonised peoples in Africa and Indigenous peoples elsewhere. Earlier, Chilisa and colleagues (2016) proposed an African relational evaluation paradigm and an African evaluation tree.

Method note. This family tree is our adaptation of a published framework, the evaluation theory tree of Marvin Alkin and Christina Christie, introduced in Evaluation Roots (2004) and revised in later editions (a second edition, then a third in 2023, which classifies theories rather than theorists). Placements marked as Alkin and Christie's are inferred from the part of Evaluation Roots in which a theorist's chapter appears (Methods, Valuing or Use), as listed by SAGE for the 2004 book, or from the 2023 Guilford contents for Greene, Chen and Preskill; the tree's own diagram was not consulted directly, so leaf positions within a branch are not reproduced. The social justice branch is Mertens's revision, not Alkin and Christie's. The context and needs branch is a proposal by Mertens and Chilisa, not an accepted part of the tree. Edges summarise sourced relationships; where a note says our reading, the connection is interpretive. 11 · Glossary

Key terms in plain words

20 terms you will meet in the stories, from counterfactual to transformative paradigm.

20 terms
Change model and action model
In theory-driven evaluation, the change model describes how an intervention produces outcomes; the action model describes how the programme must be delivered to make that happen.
CIPP model
Context, Input, Process, Product: four linked evaluation questions designed to serve decisions throughout a programme.
Coefficient alpha
A statistic estimating how consistently a set of survey or test items measures something. It is a lower bound on reliability and does not prove a scale measures a single thing.
Counterfactual
What would have happened without the programme. Impact evaluations estimate it, often with a comparison or control group.
Developmental evaluation
Evaluation that works alongside innovators in complex, changing situations, giving real-time feedback to support adaptation.
Evidence gap map
A visual summary of where evidence on a set of interventions and outcomes exists and where it is missing.
Formative evaluation
Evaluation done to improve something while it is being developed or delivered.
Goal-free evaluation
Judging what a programme actually does against the needs of those it serves, without being steered by its stated goals.
Made in Africa Evaluation
The call for evaluation in Africa to be grounded in African philosophies, contexts and values and led by African evaluators.
Most Significant Change (MSC)
A participatory monitoring technique in which stories of change are collected and then discussed and selected at different levels, revealing what stakeholders value.
Participatory rural appraisal (PRA)
A family of visual, group-based methods, such as mapping, transect walks and wealth ranking, through which local people produce and analyse their own information.
Quasi-experimental design
A design that compares groups or time periods without random assignment, while ruling out rival explanations as far as possible.
Randomised evaluation
An impact design in which chance, such as a lottery, decides who receives a programme, so differences in outcomes can be credited to it.
Responsive evaluation
Evaluation organised around the concerns and issues of people connected with a programme, often using case study, and reported in forms they can use.
Summative evaluation
Evaluation that judges the overall worth of something, usually to inform a decision about it.
Theory of change
An explicit account of how and why a programme's activities are expected to lead to its intended outcomes, including the assumptions behind each step.
Threats to validity
Rival explanations or weaknesses that could make a causal claim, or its generalisation, wrong.
Transformative paradigm
A framework that centres people facing discrimination or oppression, attends to power and designs research and evaluation to support social justice.
Trustworthiness
In naturalistic inquiry, the set of criteria used to judge the quality of qualitative work in place of conventional validity and reliability.
Utilization-focused evaluation
An approach that judges evaluations by whether intended users actually use them, and designs everything around those users and uses.

12 · Method

An honest note on method

How the book was curated, how every fact was checked, how AI was used, and what the book is and is not.

Facts checked as of October 2026

01Why we made this book

Every tool in your MEL kit was once somebody's answer to a hard question. We curated this book to celebrate the people who asked those questions first, to make their big ideas clear enough for anyone to use, and to inspire a new generation to join the work. Behind it sits one purpose: a stronger evaluation ecosystem, of practitioners, students, commissioners, governments and communities, using evidence well to help build a fairer and better world.

02Who made it

The book was curated by Shashidhar S A, founder of HubForge Global, and is published by HubForge Global Impact Network Foundation. The curator chose the 24 figures, set the brief for every chapter, directed the writing and design through many rounds of revision, and reviewed and approved the final text. The choices of who to include, what to emphasise and how to tell each story are ours, and so is the responsibility for them.

03How we chose the 24

We chose the 24 to show how the field grew, not to rank anyone. We looked for people whose ideas changed everyday practice, across different eras, regions, methods and roles, from university theorists to field practitioners and movement builders, with a deliberate place for voices from Africa and South Asia. Many others shaped the field, and we hope future editions will tell more of their stories.

04Where the facts come from

Each profile was built from a research fact sheet compiled from public sources: university and institutional pages, publishers' author pages, professional association records such as the American Evaluation Association's award lists, encyclopaedia entries, obituaries and the person's own published work. More than 300 of these sources are listed in Notes and sources, from <a class="pref" data-ref="notes" data-fmt="p">p. 000</a>, as a starting point for your own reading rather than a complete bibliography. Where sources disagreed on a date or detail, we left it out or hedged it, for example by writing around 1990 or the mid-1960s. Where a fact rested on one weak source, or could not be confirmed, we left it out.

05How we used AI

We used artificial intelligence openly in making this book. AI tools helped us to search and summarise public sources, draft and edit the text, check consistency and lay out the pages, always under the curator's direction. We did not let AI supply facts of its own: every fact in the book is meant to trace back to a listed public source. We ran automated checks that every number, date and name in the rewritten chapters already appeared in the sourced research, followed by a separate fact-checking pass that corrected errors and removed claims the sources did not support. AI can still make mistakes, and so can people, which is why we invite corrections.

06How we tell the stories

The storytelling is ours. Chapter openings, passages that invite you to imagine a situation, the Picture it comparisons, the Your moves cards, the worked examples, templates, AI prompts and reflection questions are HubForge's own writing and interpretation. They are not claims about what any person said, believes or would recommend. Worked examples are invented for teaching unless a source is named. We did not invent scenes, dialogue, thoughts or feelings, and we deliberately avoided direct quotations, because quotations are often misremembered, trimmed or lifted out of context. Instead, we describe each person's ideas in our own words and point you to their original work.

07Portraits and emblems

Five figures are shown by portraits adapted from openly licensed photographs: cropped, traced into vector shapes and recoloured, and credited in Picture credits, from <a class="pref" data-ref="credits" data-fmt="p">p. 000</a>. Every other figure is shown by an original HubForge emblem that stands for their big idea. The emblems are not likenesses.

08Respect for the people

We have not stated whether a person is living unless that was clearly confirmed, we have kept current job titles to a minimum, and we have kept to each person's public professional record. Criticisms are summaries of published debate, offered in good faith for learning, not personal judgements. Inclusion in this book is not an endorsement by the people profiled, their families or their institutions.

09Corrections and limits

Facts were checked against public sources as of October 2026. If you spot an error, an omission or an outdated detail, or if you are a person profiled and want something corrected, write to hubforgeglobal@gmail.com and we will review it promptly and fix the next edition. Why this book

Shashidhar has long loved the lives, ideas, experiments and breakthrough moments of scientists, mathematicians and entrepreneurs: the stories behind original thinking, and the way those stories inspire people. He curated Giants of Evidence to bring that kind of story to monitoring, evaluation and learning, to celebrate the people who built the field, and to help a new generation ask better questions of its own.

An engineer by training, Shashidhar has spent about a decade in monitoring, evaluation and learning across India's social sector. His path runs through seven roles, from grassroots field work to state-level government partnerships and the design of MEL systems.

In the global evaluation community

An active volunteer, contributing to IOCE, the International Organization for Cooperation in Evaluation, and to Eval4Action, working alongside the EvalYouth Global Network.
